## Start here — when would you use worldbuilding?

Suppose you imagine a beautiful city where people keep their memories inside glowing lanterns.

You have an idea. But imagine you want to understand **what living in that city might actually feel like**. A picture of the lanterns cannot answer every question on its own.

You could begin with just one:

**If memories are stored in lanterns, what might change about a person's everyday choices?**

This is a useful moment for worldbuilding. Instead of inventing an entire planet, you explore how one imagined condition affects something else.

![Diagram showing a single idea about memory lanterns becoming a consent rule, a teacher's choice, and a consequence for a school lesson.](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/Reltroner/lms-reltroner-resource@c6f0263ca0ae888d02e87450b8d8a9a9351123ac/courses/worldbuilding-operating-system/module-01-worldbuilding-fundamentals/01-01-what-is-worldbuilding/01-01-idea-to-world.svg)

*Visual 1 — Idea → condition → choice → possible effect. One imagined idea can lead to a condition, a choice, and an effect. Follow the steps from top to bottom; each step should have a reason.*

**What changed?** We moved from describing an object to asking how the world around that object works.

## What is worldbuilding?

**Worldbuilding is the creative process of imagining and developing a fictional world: its conditions, places, inhabitants, relationships, and possible consequences.**

Worldbuilding can support a novel, illustration, game, comic, role-playing activity, or private creative project. It does not require any particular genre or medium.

A world can be one room or an enormous galaxy. The size is not the lesson's success measure. One understandable relationship between two elements is enough to begin exploring it.

Four questions can help:

![Diagram of four optional worldbuilding questions: Where does it happen? Under what conditions? Who is affected? What changes?](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/Reltroner/lms-reltroner-resource@c6f0263ca0ae888d02e87450b8d8a9a9351123ac/courses/worldbuilding-operating-system/module-01-worldbuilding-fundamentals/01-01-what-is-worldbuilding/01-01-four-questions.svg)

*Visual 2 — Four possible questions for exploring an imagined world. You do not need to answer all four today.*

- **Where?** Is the idea happening in one house, a village, or a city?
- **Under what conditions?** What is possible, limited, or costly there?
- **Who is affected?** Which person or group has a decision to make?
- **What changes?** What becomes different because of that decision?

These questions reveal a small but useful way to think: **conditions shape available choices, and choices can have consequences.**

## A small world you can examine: Lumenhaven

Our teaching example is an invented city called **Lumenhaven**. It is **not official Asthortera canon**, and you do not need to build a similar world.

Imagine these statements:

1. **Fictional idea:** lanterns can hold a person's memories.
2. **Fictional condition:** sharing a stored memory requires its owner's permission.
3. **Situation:** a teacher wants to use a memory recording in a classroom.
4. **Choice:** when the owner refuses, the teacher chooses a different account of the event.
5. **Possible effect:** the class learns from a different source.

We have not written a long history or designed the entire education system. But we can explain **why** a choice happened: the fictional sharing condition made a difference.

![Comparison diagram showing an isolated fact about memory lanterns and a connected worldbuilding explanation in which a consent rule affects a teacher's lesson.](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/Reltroner/lms-reltroner-resource@c6f0263ca0ae888d02e87450b8d8a9a9351123ac/courses/worldbuilding-operating-system/module-01-worldbuilding-fundamentals/01-01-what-is-worldbuilding/01-01-fact-to-consequence.svg)

*Visual 3 — A world fact can become part of a meaningful explanation. Isolated facts are valid creative seeds; connections help show what they affect.*

## Your first mental model — condition, choice, effect

A **mental model** is a simple way to organize your thinking about how something works. It is useful when it helps you ask better questions, not when it merely gives a complex name to an obvious observation.

For this lesson, try one small model:

**Condition → Choice → Possible effect**

In Lumenhaven:

- **Condition:** sharing a memory needs permission.
- **Choice:** the teacher changes the source used for class.
- **Possible effect:** students encounter a different account.

Notice the word **possible**. Within your own fictional world, you may decide which rules hold. The effect still needs a reasonable explanation under those rules.

This pattern is **not a guarantee** that every choice has one predictable effect. Other conditions and people may matter. Today's goal is simply to recognize and explain one plausible connection.

### Use the model to ask a better question

Suppose the teacher never asked the owner for permission. Would the classroom outcome stay the same? What information would you need in your fictional world to decide?

You can now see two ways of thinking:

- **Just adding a fact:** "There are ten memory lanterns in the classroom."
- **Investigating a condition:** "If permission is denied, what can the teacher do, and why?"

Neither sentence is forbidden. The second offers a more direct route to examining a decision and its consequences.

## Where else might this way of thinking help?

The **fictional details do not transfer to real life**. Memory lanterns are imaginary. But the *questioning pattern* might be useful in an ordinary situation.

For example, a team preparing a small presentation may need a draft before they can rehearse. If the draft is unavailable, the next step may need to change.

**From fiction to everyday reasoning — transfer the question, not the imagined answer:**

1. **Fictional context:** a teacher in Lumenhaven needs permission to use a memory.
2. **Abstract pattern:** an available choice depends on a condition that may not be met.
3. **Possible everyday analogy:** a presentation team may need a draft before it can rehearse.
4. **Limits:** separate what may be similar from what must be checked in the real situation.

Here is the careful comparison:

**The situation**

- *In the fictional world:* A teacher needs permission to use a memory.
- *In an everyday situation:* A presentation group may need a teammate's draft before rehearsal.

**The shared pattern**

- *In the fictional world:* A missing prerequisite changes an available choice.
- *In an everyday situation:* Missing material may change the group's next step.

**Where the rules come from**

- *In the fictional world:* The rules of memory lanterns are invented by the creator.
- *In an everyday situation:* Actual deadlines, responsibilities, and alternatives must be **checked**, not invented.

**What could be useful?** Asking which condition a task depends on, and what choices remain if that condition changes.

**What does not transfer?** The fictional lantern, the imagined consent mechanism, and assumptions about how real teammates behave.

**What is still unknown?** Whether the real draft is required, when it will arrive, and what fallback is acceptable to the people involved.

A real problem needs real information. You can **recognize a similar pattern** without claiming that you have solved the real problem. Sometimes a comparison is not useful at all; recognizing a bad analogy is also good reasoning.

## Try it yourself — your first worldbuilding observation

A notebook, plain-text file, or a few lines on paper are enough. You are not being asked to build an entire city.

1. **Choose a seed.** Imagine one object, place, person, custom, or unusual phenomenon.
2. **Choose one condition.** What must be true, unavailable, permitted, or limited for that seed to matter?
3. **Give someone a choice.** What would a person do differently because of the condition?
4. **Name one possible effect.** What might change, and why?

Copy the following if helpful:

```text
MY FIRST WORLDBUILDING OBSERVATION

My imagined idea:

One condition or rule:

A person or group making a choice:

One possible effect:

This effect follows because:
```

### Worked example — a different world

- **Idea:** streetlights in a town react to residents' dreams.
- **Condition:** widespread nightmares dim the streetlights.
- **Choice:** a shopkeeper opens later than usual.
- **Possible effect:** fewer shops operate during the early morning.
- **Because:** the changing light conditions affect when people feel able to travel or work.

This is one coherent possibility under the imagined rules, not the only valid outcome.

### Optional: the pattern bridge

Only if it interests you, add **two short lines** to your note:

```text
A similar kind of question in everyday life might be:

One important difference or fact I would need to check is:
```

For example, an ordinary event plan may depend on a room being available. **But** real room availability and participant needs must be confirmed; they are not worldbuilding rules that you can simply declare.

The optional bridge is **pattern recognition**, not a requirement to make a real-world decision or prove a skill.

## Check your understanding

**1. Is a beautiful drawing of a fictional city a valid starting point?**

Yes. Visual imagination is a legitimate seed. This lesson offers a way to **explore what the city changes**, not a rule that art without causal notes is worthless.

**2. Which explanation makes an actual relationship visible?**

- **A:** "The town has seven towers."
- **B:** "One tower supplies the town's water, so its closure may change residents' daily routines."

**B** makes a condition and possible effect explicit. **A** could become meaningful through later exploration too.

**3. What is the mental model in today's teaching example?**

**Condition → Choice → Possible effect.** It helps you look for a relationship instead of only collecting details.

**4. If a project task depends on a teammate's draft, can you assume the same rules as Lumenhaven's memory lanterns?**

No. The **dependency question** may be similar, but the real deadline, people, obligations, and alternatives need evidence. A fictional rule is not evidence about an actual situation.

### Before you continue — self-check

- I can describe one imaginative idea without building a whole universe.
- I can identify one condition, one human choice, and one possible effect.
- I can name the pattern without referring to memory lanterns.
- If I suggest an everyday analogy, I can say what does not map or remains unknown.

## What you take forward

Your required output is **My First Worldbuilding Observation**: one imagined idea, one condition, a choice, and a plausible connection you can explain.

You may also keep an **Optional Pattern Bridge**: one everyday question that resembles the reasoning pattern, and one important difference. This is a first observation, **not a claim of independent cross-domain mastery**.

**Key takeaway:** Worldbuilding gives you a place to practice asking **what a condition changes, why a choice follows, and which consequences might matter**. The thinking pattern can become useful elsewhere **only after you check the new context**.
